How to Choose a Portable SSD for Video Editing

Before buying a drive, you need to understand how technical specifications translate to real-world editing performance.

Speed: Sustained vs. Peak Performance

Manufacturers love to print peak speeds on their packaging—often around 1,000 MB/s or 2,000 MB/s. However, these speeds usually only last for a few seconds during a short burst transfer. When you write a 100GB video project file, the drive’s cache fills up, and performance can drop significantly. Look for reviews or drives that maintain high sustained write speeds so your exports and long file copies don't slow to a crawl.

Connection Interfaces

Your drive is only as fast as the port you plug it into.

  • USB 3.2 Gen 2 (up to 1,050 MB/s): Perfect for standard 4K editing workflows, proxies, and multi-stream timelines.
  • USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 or USB4 / Thunderbolt (up to 2,000 MB/s+): Ideal if you edit directly off the drive with raw 4K/8K footage or massive project libraries. Ensure your computer actually supports these faster standards before paying extra for them.

Capacity Needs

Video files are massive. A 1TB drive sounds large until you load it with raw camera footage. For serious video editing, 2TB is the practical sweet spot for a working project drive, while 4TB or higher is great for extensive archives. Avoid 500GB drives entirely; they fill up too quickly and often have slower write speeds than their larger-capacity counterparts.

Thermal Management

Editing directly from an external SSD generates heat. If a drive gets too hot, it will thermal throttle, drastically reducing its speed to cool down. Drives with aluminum casings or built-in heat dissipation generally handle long rendering sessions better than sealed rubberized blocks.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Editing off a mechanical hard drive (HDD): Traditional spinning hard drives are too slow for modern video editing timelines. Always use an SSD for active projects.
  • Ignoring your computer's port limits: Buying a 2,000 MB/s USB-C drive is a waste of money if your laptop only has older USB ports that cap out at 500 MB/s. Match your drive to your hardware capabilities.
  • Using your working drive as your only backup: An SSD is a working canvas, not an archival vault. Always maintain a separate backup copy of your raw footage and project files on a separate physical drive or cloud storage.
  • Buying capacities that are too small: A 500GB drive will fill up instantly once you factor in cache files, scratch disks, and raw video clips. Aim for 2TB minimum.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I edit 4K video directly from a portable SSD?

Yes. Modern portable SSDs with read/write speeds of 1,050 MB/s or higher can easily stream 4K video files directly on your timeline without lagging or dropping frames.

Do I need a Thunderbolt SSD or is USB-C enough?

For 90% of video editors, a fast USB-C SSD (USB 3.2 Gen 2 or Gen 2x2) is more than fast enough. Thunderbolt drives are generally more expensive and run hotter, making them necessary mainly for specialized high-end multi-stream 8K workflows.

How do I format my new SSD for video editing?

If you use a Mac exclusively, format it to APFS for the best performance. If you switch between Windows and Mac computers, format it to exFAT so both operating systems can read and write to the drive natively without third-party software.
